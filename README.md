<!-- BLOG-POST-LIST:START -->- [Target x WiCyS Cyber Defense Challenge - Lessons from 2nd Place](https://aerobytes.io/writeups/target-tier-3-retrospective/) - &lt;p&gt;&lt;img src=&quot;/assets/images/tier3.png&quot; alt=&quot;Tier 3 Badge&quot; /&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h1 id=&quot;2025-target-x-wicys-cyber-defense-challenge&quot;&gt;2025 Target x WiCyS Cyber Defense Challenge&lt;/h1&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;tier-3-presentation-summary&quot;&gt;Tier 3 Presentation Summary&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;2nd Place Overall&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;hr /&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;overview-of-the-2025-target-x-wicys-cyber-defense-challenge&quot;&gt;Overview of the 2025 Target x WiCyS Cyber Defense Challenge&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The challenge centered around a fictional breach at Personalyz.io, walking participants through both sides of the attack. Tier 1 put me on defense, responding to an extortion threat. I tracked down the sender through email headers, dug through logs for evidence, and even had to negotiate with the threat actor to figure out if the breach was legit. From there it was full forensics work: Wireshark to identify the compromised host and C2 server, decoding DNS exfiltration, combing through process and command history to find the malicious program, and discovering a tampered backup app plus remnants of a wiped system.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Tier 2 switched perspectives entirely and put me in the attacker role. I got in through a weaponized resume, exploited a template engine vulnerability, moved laterally using file inclusion and command execution, escalated out of a restricted shell, and pulled sensitive files by enumerating LDAP and SMB. The final challenge had me reverse engineer a DNS-over-HTTPS exfiltration client that was smuggling data out in encrypted traffic. Once I saw how it actually worked, everything I’d been piecing together defensively in Tier 1 suddenly clicked.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;what-i-learned&quot;&gt;What I Learned&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Working through both tiers gave me a much better understanding of how real attacks actually unfold. Tier 1 taught me to be methodical about incident response. I learned to slow down, build timelines from different data sources &lpar;email headers, logs, packet captures, endpoint artifacts&rpar;, and let the technical details tell the story instead of jumping to conclusions. Even the negotiation part was valuable. Knowing how to communicate clearly and present evidence ended up being just as important as the technical work.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;But Tier 2 is where I learned the most. I’d spent almost all my time on the defensive side before this, so offensive work was completely new territory. Getting hands-on with exploiting vulnerabilities, moving through a network, escalating privileges, and enumerating services taught me how attackers actually think and move. Reversing that DoH exfiltration client was the moment everything came together. I finally understood exactly how the attacker had engineered what I’d been analyzing from the defensive side in Tier 1.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Seeing both perspectives changed how I approach security. I understand now how small misconfigurations can be chained together, what kind of traces attackers leave &lpar;even when they’re trying not to&rpar;, and how to spot those traces faster. It’s made me a better analyst.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;how-this-will-help-in-my-career&quot;&gt;How This Will Help in My Career&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;This challenge came at a good time for me career-wise. My path into tech has been anything but traditional. I’ve always been a gamer/techie at heart &lpar;aka unofficial family tech support&rpar;, but I studied music in college and started out as a band director. Then I joined the Army as a geospatial intelligence analyst, and now I’m making the jump into cybersecurity. Each step taught me different things about discipline, how to communicate clearly, and how to break down complex problems. This challenge showed me those skills transfer well to cybersecurity work.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Tier 1 felt familiar in a lot of ways. The analytical work of digging through logs, connecting scattered clues, verifying evidence, and building a clear picture from messy data closely mirrors what I did in intelligence work. It was good to see that the problem-solving approach I developed there works just as well for incident response.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Tier 2 pushed me the most. Coming from a defensive background with basically zero offensive experience, learning to think like an attacker was completely new. But understanding how an intrusion actually happens from the attacker’s perspective made everything click. Now I know what attackers are looking for and how they exploit things, which makes me better at defense.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Getting through both tiers proved something important to me: I can pick up new technical skills quickly, adapt when things get hard, and handle challenges I’ve never seen before. It also gave me something concrete to point to when I’m explaining my career transition. My background might not be traditional, but that’s turned out to be an advantage. This challenge gave me the experience and confidence I needed to keep pushing forward in cybersecurity.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;things-i-wish-i-knew-before-starting&quot;&gt;Things I Wish I Knew Before Starting&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;I underestimated how quickly the difficulty would ramp up. The first few challenges are approachable, but the technical complexity increases fast once you hit network forensics and reverse engineering. When I got stuck, I found that stepping away and coming back with fresh eyes was often the best strategy to break through a problem, even if I thought I was close to the solution.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Time commitment was another factor. Many of these challenges required significant stretches of focused work &lpar;looking at you, D4, and my thousands of failed attempts&rpar;. As a stay-at-home mom, I was fortunate to have the flexibility to block out uninterrupted time when I needed it, which made a real difference in my ability to work through the more complex problems and achieve the success I did in the challenge.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;feedback-for-the-target-team&quot;&gt;Feedback for the Target Team&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The challenge was really well designed. The scenario felt realistic, the skills built on each other in a way that made sense, and getting to work through both the defensive and offensive sides made it way more valuable than a typical CTF. You can tell a lot of thought went into making something that actually teaches you instead of just testing you.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The Slack community was honestly one of the best parts. The Target team was patient and helpful, providing sanity checks when I was stuck on the harder challenges. Special shoutout to Geoff &lpar;Zeke&rpar; for entertaining my wild, overcomplicated approaches to some of these challenges.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Overall, this was exactly what I needed at this point in my career. It pushed me hard, taught me a great deal, and gave me skills I’ll be using for a long time.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h1 id=&quot;2025-target-cyber-defense-challenge-when-all-exits-are-blocked-go-underground&quot;&gt;2025 Target Cyber Defense Challenge: When All Exits Are Blocked, Go Underground&lt;/h1&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Target Corporation x Women in Cybersecurity &lpar;WiCyS&rpar;&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;🏆 2nd Place Overall 🏆&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;o5-tunnel-vision&quot;&gt;O5: Tunnel Vision&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;em&gt;Reverse engineering a DNS exfiltration binary when every other escape route has been cut off&lt;/em&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;hr /&gt;

&lt;p&gt;While the competition had multiple challenges, I’m writing about O5: Tunnel Vision because of its complexity and my learning journey. Starting with zero reverse engineering experience, I had to quickly learn Ghidra and static binary analysis.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The solution took two weeks of dedicated focus - analyzing ARM64 assembly and writing Python scripts to test protocol theories. When scripts threw errors &lpar;often&rpar;, I used AI help to debug and keep moving. I spent an entire week just trying to decrypt the server’s cryptic error messages before I could use them as feedback to improve my client.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;I went from fumbling through assembly code to confidently rebuilding a complete cryptographic protocol. I wanted to document this journey for others starting from the same place.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;hr /&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;the-setup-last-resort-protocols&quot;&gt;The Setup: Last Resort Protocols&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;em&gt;“You’ve tried every exit. Proxies clamped shut. Egress rules got strict. The SOC started tracing anything that looks even a little suspicious.”&lt;/em&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;This was O5: Tunnel Vision, a 500-point CTF challenge that put us in the shoes of an operator whose usual exfiltration methods had been blocked. With network monitoring tightened and suspicious traffic being traced, we needed to fall back to more subtle techniques.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;A friend throws us a lifeline: an old service binary from a previous DNS exfiltration job. No documentation. No source code. No memory of the handshake protocol. Just a black-box binary and the knowledge that somewhere inside its compiled assembly lies a working covert channel.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;The Mission&lt;/strong&gt;:&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;ul&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Reverse engineer the unknown protocol from the binary alone&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Build a client that can speak the wire protocol correctly&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Successfully upload a file through the DNS covert channel&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Extract the flag from the server’s response&lt;/li&gt;
&lt;/ul&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;The Resources&lt;/strong&gt;:&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;ul&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Target endpoint&lt;/strong&gt;: &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;https://target-exfil.chals.io&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Exfiltration domain&lt;/strong&gt;: &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;xfl.tn&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Upload file&lt;/strong&gt;: Provided binary file that must be transmitted&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Server binary&lt;/strong&gt;: ARM64 Go executable with no source or documentation&lt;/li&gt;
&lt;/ul&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The server binary was ARM64 Go 1.25 &lpar;released just a week before the competition&rpar;, making local debugging seemingly impossible for most competitors, unless they had a compatible Mac. Since I own 0 Apple products, everything had to be done through static analysis in Ghidra - no dynamic analysis, no local testing, just assembly analysis and testing guesses against the live server.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The organizers eventually released an x86_64 version to help other competitors run the binary on a local server, but this solution was developed entirely through static analysis of the ARM64 binary.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Main Challenge Components:&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;ul&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Go binary server &lpar;ARM64 architecture&rpar;&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;DNS-over-HTTPS covert channel using AAAA records&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;NaCl &lpar;Network and Cryptography library&rpar; encryption&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Base32 hex encoding for DNS-safe transmission&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Multi-chunk file upload protocol&lt;/li&gt;
&lt;/ul&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;initial-analysis-with-ghidra&quot;&gt;Initial Analysis with Ghidra&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;what-we-found-main-function-structure&quot;&gt;What We Found: Main Function Structure&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The first breakthrough came from analyzing the strings in the binary. Using Ghidra’s string search turned up several important clues:&lt;/p&gt;
&lt;div class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;&lt;div class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;pre class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;code&gt;// From exfil analysis
&quot;exfil/dns.go&quot;
&quot;processChunk0&quot;
&quot;processChunkN&quot; 
&quot;handleUploadRequest&quot;
&quot;github.com/miekg/dns&quot;
&quot;encoding/base32&quot;
&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/pre&gt;&lt;/div&gt;&lt;/div&gt;

&lt;p&gt;These strings immediately told us we were dealing with:&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;ul&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;A DNS-based system &lpar;using the popular &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;miekg/dns&lt;/code&gt; Go library&rpar;&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Chunk-based data transfer&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Base32 encoding for DNS compatibility&lt;/li&gt;
&lt;/ul&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;function-analysis-the-core-protocol-handlers&quot;&gt;Function Analysis: The Core Protocol Handlers&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;h4 id=&quot;1-maindnshandlerprocesschunk0&quot;&gt;1. &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.processChunk0&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/h4&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Located at address &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;10021f840&lt;/code&gt;, this function handles the initial chunk &lpar;chunk 0&rpar; of the upload process. Important observations from the Ghidra decompilation:&lt;/p&gt;
&lt;div class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;&lt;div class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;pre class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;code&gt;// From main.processChunk0
void main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.processChunk0&lpar;main.DNSHandler *this, ...&rpar;
{
    // ... stack setup ...
    
    // Key crypto operations visible:
    bl main.DecryptWithSharedKey  // Decrypt incoming data
    
    // Process the decrypted chunk
    bl main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.processChunk0 
    
    // Respond based on processing result
    bl main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.respondWithSuccess
}
&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/pre&gt;&lt;/div&gt;&lt;/div&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The function clearly shows a decrypt-process-respond pattern.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h4 id=&quot;2-maindnshandlerprocesschunkn&quot;&gt;2. &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.processChunkN&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/h4&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Located at address &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;10021fbb0&lt;/code&gt;, this handles subsequent chunks &lpar;N &amp;gt; 0&rpar;. From the analysis in &lt;code class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;main.processChunkN&lt;/code&gt;:&lt;/p&gt;
&lt;div class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;&lt;div class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;pre class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;code&gt;void main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.processChunkN&lpar;main.DNSHandler *this, ...&rpar;
{
    // ... setup code ...
    
    // Decision logic based on chunk processing
    if &lpar;condition&rpar; {
        main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.respondWithFinished&lpar;this,param_2,param_3&rpar;;
    }
    else {
        main.&lpar;*DNSHandler&rpar;.respondWithSuccess&lpar;this,param_2,iVar13,param_3&rpar;;
    }
}
&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/pre&gt;&lt;/div&gt;&lt;/div&gt;

&lt;p&gt;This revealed the protocol’s completion mechanism - the server sends a “finished” response when all chunks are received.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;cryptographic-implementation-findings&quot;&gt;Cryptographic Implementation Findings&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;h4 id=&quot;nacl-secretbox-usage&quot;&gt;NaCl Secretbox Usage&lt;/h4&gt;

&lt;p&gt;The binary analysis showed extensive use of NaCl cryptographic functions:&lt;/p&gt;
&lt;div class=&quot;language-plaintext highlighter-rouge&quot;&gt;&lt;div class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;pre class=&quot;highlight&quot;&gt;&lt;code&gt;// From secretbox.Open and secretbox.Seal
bl golang.org/x/crypto/nacl/secretbox.Seal
bl golang.org/x/crypto/nacl/secretbox.Open
&lt;/code&gt;&lt;/pre&gt;&lt;/div&gt;&lt;/div&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;security-considerations-i-learned&quot;&gt;Security Considerations I Learned&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Building this project taught me several important security lessons about blockchain:&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;immutability-is-permanent&quot;&gt;Immutability is Permanent&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Once deployed, smart contracts cannot be modified. If there’s a bug in the code, you can’t just patch it like traditional software. This makes pre-deployment security auditing something you can’t skip.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;private-keys&quot;&gt;Private Keys&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Your private key is your identity on the blockchain. If someone gets your private key, they control your wallet and any contracts you’ve deployed. I learned to:&lt;/p&gt;
&lt;ul&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Never commit private keys to version control&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Use environment variables for sensitive data&lt;/li&gt;
  &lt;li&gt;Keep testnet and mainnet keys completely separate&lt;/li&gt;
&lt;/ul&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;gas-costs&quot;&gt;Gas Costs&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Every operation on Ethereum costs gas &lpar;transaction fees&rpar;. Inefficient code doesn’t just run slowly, it costs real money. This forces developers to optimize in ways traditional programming doesn’t require.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;common-attack-vectors&quot;&gt;Common Attack Vectors&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;p&gt;While my simple token isn’t vulnerable to reentrancy attacks, learning about these common smart contract exploits showed me how attackers think. Understanding vulnerabilities like reentrancy attacks &lpar;the DAO hack&rpar;, integer overflow/underflow, front-running, and access control issues helps me recognize how DeFi protocols get exploited.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h3 id=&quot;everything-is-public&quot;&gt;Everything is Public&lt;/h3&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Everything on a public blockchain is transparent. All transactions, all balances, all contract interactions are publicly visible. This has huge implications for privacy and operational security.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;how-this-relates-to-cybersecurity&quot;&gt;How This Relates to Cybersecurity&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Understanding blockchain isn’t just about cryptocurrency. From a security perspective, this knowledge helps with:&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Incident Response&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;When organizations get hit with crypto ransomware or crypto-mining malware, understanding how blockchain transactions work helps trace and analyze the attack.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Threat Intelligence&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Many threat actors use cryptocurrency for payments and money laundering. Understanding blockchain forensics helps track criminal activity.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Smart Contract Auditing&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;DeFi is a massive attack surface. Knowing how to read Solidity code and identify vulnerabilities is a valuable skill for security researchers.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;&lt;strong&gt;Web3 Security&lt;/strong&gt;&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;As more applications integrate blockchain technology, understanding the security implications becomes increasingly important.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;h2 id=&quot;lessons-learned&quot;&gt;Lessons Learned&lt;/h2&gt;

&lt;p&gt;This project reinforced several important principles:&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Start with tested libraries. Don’t reinvent the wheel, especially in security code. OpenZeppelin’s contracts are audited and widely used for good reason.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Testing needs to be thorough. In blockchain, mistakes are permanent and potentially expensive. You can’t skip this step.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Understand the fundamentals. You can’t secure something you don’t understand. Building this from scratch forced me to learn how Ethereum actually works.&lt;/p&gt;

&lt;p&gt;Clear documentation and code comments are essential, especially when code is publicly verifiable on-chain.&lt;/p&gt;
